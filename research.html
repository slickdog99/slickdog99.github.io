Research

I am a B.Tech Computer Engineering student at DJSCE, Mumbai, graduating in 2028, working toward research in mechanistic interpretability and technical AI safety. What pulls me toward it is plain curiosity: we build these models and still cannot read them. I want to find out how far interpretability can actually go — how much of what a model is doing inside can be made legible, named, and checked.

My current work audits a generate-and-screen pipeline in protein design. It asks whether a published steering method acts through its intended target, whether a structure predictor's confidence score measures what practitioners assume it measures, and whether an activation probe that successfully predicts a model's own verdict tells you anything about physical reality. Largely it does not — and the paper reports that rather than the result we would have preferred to find.

The Confessions of a Protein Language Model: Steering, Activations, and What Predicted Confidence Cannot Certify

Panini Shah*, Raj Panchali*, Pearl Mody, Mahek Sanghvi, Meera Narvekar, Ruhina Karani

*Equal contribution

bioRxiv ↗ PDF ↓ code ↗

Summary
Protein generate-and-screen pipelines use an intervention to shape candidates and a cheap readout to select them, but rarely test whether either tracks its intended target. We audit three links. Steering preserves structural confidence only below a measured dose threshold (α50 = 1.240×); above saturation an arbitrary matched-norm direction does equal damage, turning concept control into magnitude. The confidence score has an axis of its own — pLDDT rises under truncation — so an intervention that shortens its output can appear inert. Activations do predict the confidence gate early enough to abandon a candidate mid-sequence and save roughly 27% of pipeline compute at 81% recall. That usefulness stops there: on 433 length- and composition-matched design–scramble pairs with measured protease stability, no balanced-accuracy interval excludes chance. Predicting the gate does not establish foldability.
